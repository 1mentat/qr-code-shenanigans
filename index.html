Color photo QR codes

Extending Andrew Taylor's dithered QR codes from one-bit halftones to full-color photographs — every code below scans, straight off this screen.

The two freedoms scanners give you

Taylor's technique exploits one freedom: a scanner samples only the center of each module, so everything outside a center dot is yours. His generator spends that freedom on a Floyd–Steinberg halftone, with a clever two-pass error diffusion that hides the forced data modules inside the dither texture.

There's a second freedom sitting next to it: scanners are colorblind. Every decoder flattens the image to grayscale before thresholding — but different decoders flatten differently. Some use Rec.601 luma, some average channels, some just take green. All of these are convex combinations of R, G, B, which yields a guarantee:

If every channel of a dark center is below the local threshold, and every channel of a light center is above it, the module reads correctly under any grayscale conversion. Hue and saturation never enter into it — they're free for the photo.

Darkening a pixel by multiplicative scaling keeps its hue and saturation; lightening by blending toward white keeps its hue. So the forced dots themselves stay in the photo's palette — look at the landscape code above: the dots in the river are navy, not black.

The pipeline

  1. Soft round dots, not square modules. Only a dot of radius 0.36 module-widths is fully forced; a smoothstep ring fades the constraint to zero by 0.50. Where the photo already satisfies the bound, forcing is a no-op and the dot vanishes — dark areas of the photo swallow their dark dots entirely.
  2. Channel-bound forcing. Dark centers: scale RGB so max(R,G,B) ≤ 0.30. Light centers: blend toward white so min(R,G,B) ≥ 0.72. Function patterns get tighter bounds (0.14 / 0.88) but the same hue-preserving treatment, so even the finder squares pick up a tint.
  3. Two-pass error diffusion, continuous-tone edition. Taylor pre-diffuses the error from forced data modules into the surrounding halftone. Here there's no halftone — neighbors are continuous-tone, so they can absorb error directly: the luminance the dots inject is Gaussian-spread over nearby unconstrained pixels and subtracted from their luminance (chroma untouched). Local average brightness still tracks the photo, so at viewing distance the grid melts away.
  4. Mask selection. All 8 QR mask patterns are generated and scored against the photo's per-module luminance; the closest match wins. A cheap cousin of QArt-style codeword steering — worth a few percent of dots that were going to agree with the photo anyway.
  5. Photo-continued quiet zone. The mandatory margin shows the photo blended toward white (min channel ≥ 0.78) instead of dead space.

Error correction is level H, and none of it is spent at generation time — the centers are all genuinely correct — so the full 30% redundancy stays in reserve for glare, folds, and potato phones.

Does it actually scan?

Tested locally with two independent decoders (zxing-cpp and OpenCV's QRCodeDetector) under a 20-way degradation matrix. All three photos pass every case:

The parameter sweep found something worth knowing: dot size, not contrast, is the binding constraint. With hard dots at 0.32 module-radius, aggressive downscaling failed even at maximum contrast; at 0.36, everything passes even with the mildest color bounds — which is exactly the trade you want, since dot size costs less visually than crushing the photo's tones.

Run it

uv run colorqr.py photo.jpg "https://example.com" -o out.png
uv run stress.py out.png "https://example.com"   # 20-way robustness check

Knobs: --scale (px/module), --dot-hard/--dot-soft (dot radii), --dark-max/--light-min (channel bounds), --diffuse (error-diffusion strength), --saturation, --mask.

Echoing the original article's caveat: a simulation matrix is not a stranger's phone in bad lighting. For print, raise --dot-hard and widen the channel bounds.
